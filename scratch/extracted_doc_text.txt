In the Philippines, the errand sector is transitioning into a new era of digital innovation, in which approximately three-quarters of the population were online by the start of 2024, making a large market for e-commerce and errand-based services (Kemp, 2024). The automated errand systems utilize digital platforms, such as location intelligence and real-time visibility, optimize delivery routes and reduce operational friction. For small and medium enterprises, the adaption of mobile-first management and complexity of long-distance errands services. Research indicates that roughly 93% of global organizations are now exploring or actively deploying advanced technologies to boost efficiency, indicating an unstoppable momentum towards automation in errand services (Deloitte, 2025).                       Sugo Express On the Go Logistics Services operates as of these local errand providers here in Tacurong City, serving nearby barangays since October 2020 under the Ownership of John Dominic Arellano. The business maintains a workforce of more than thirty riders who handle daily requests such as food purchases, Padala items, and bills payment across Tacurong. The dispatcher and riders are based on homes requiring contacting the rider by the dispatcher to pass the assignment to the rider. The business processed estimated One Fifty (150) transactions daily, with operation centered on a dispatcher that connects the customer to the available riders through their personal smartphones. The flat delivery fees offered by the company starts at seventy pesos (70) (From barangay Poblacion only) and an additional 5 pesos per 1 kilometer as an increase to the delivery fees. The multiple stores fees max at thirty pesos (30) per location of the store for up to 2 stores additional. An additional charge will apply for groceries maxing at fifty pesos (50) or ten percent (10%)            of the bill. For non-cod payments, they range from three thousand (3000) with a fee of fifteen pesos (15), and if the amount exceeds above, fifty pesos (50) will be applied to the payments.                        The current process of Sugo Express On the Go Logistics Services begins with customer initiation. A customer accesses the business through its official Facebook page and sends a direct message through messenger containing the service request. This initial contact includes basic information such as the full name, mobile number, type of service needed, the delivery destination, and any specific instructions regarding the request of the customer. The dispatcher, who monitors Facebook page, receives message and reviews the request details. At this stage, the dispatcher must manually verify whether the customer has used the service before by checking previous conversations or asking the customer directly. For new and existing customers, the dispatcher collects additional information including the full name, contact number, and exact address with nearby landmarks. This verification process occurs within the chat interface, with the dispatcher copying relevant details into a personal log or memorization for reference.                       Once the customer information is established, the dispatcher automatically knows when the request is part of Cash, Gcash, or Bank Transfer payment method. The customer indicates their preferred payment method from the available options which include Cash on Delivery, GCash, or Bank Transfer through Metrobank. For Cash transactions, the dispatcher notes it to its notebook the expected amount to be collected and sent this to the rider via Facebook messenger. For digital payments, the dispatcher waits for screenshot confirmation of the transfer before proceeding. In cases where the errand involves requesting on behalf of the customer, the dispatcher assesses whether the request errand value exceeds three thousand pesos (3000). High-value items require full payment verification before the rider is assigned and dispatched, while lower-value purchases may proceed and the rider collected it. The rider will collect the payment to the customer once done. The multiple store fee charges at thirty pesos (30) maximum depending on the store location (Max at 3 stores). For the Padala request, the base fee will always be the same as in the Pabili but the information collected will be different (Delivery pickup, pick up location, delivery address, location of the recipient to receive, contact number of receiver,            name of the receiver, and the name of the sender). The dispatcher will only need the picture of the item to “Padala” and judge it based on the item, if the item is heavy and the riders are not capable of accommodating that item, the dispatcher will tend to refuse the request. For Bills Payment, the base fee is always the same, but the information collects in which includes (Account information of the biller, Account number of the biller, Amount, physical receipt and/or digital receipt of the bill). The bill will collect after the assignment phase.                       Following payment verification, the dispatcher enters the assignment phase. The dispatcher maintains awareness of rider availability through a combination of his memory as a list, recent chat history, and direct messages to individual riders. Each rider communicates their status through personal messages to the dispatcher, indicating whether they are available, currently handling another errand, or offline for the day. The dispatcher reviews these informal status updates and identifies a rider who is not currently assigned to any active errand. Once a rider is selected, the dispatcher forwards the customer details through a private message, copying the information from the original customer chat. This forwarded message includes the customer’s name, contact number, delivery address, specific landmarks, request description, store location, payment method (automatically selected by the dispatcher), and expected amount, then the rider calls the customer via mobile number for the confirmation verification of the customer if it is valid or not. The dispatcher also provides any special instructions such as preferred brands, quantity limits, or time constraints.                       The rider receives the assignment and begins the transaction phase. Upon arriving at the specified store or pickup location, the rider sends a message to the dispatcher confirming arrival. The rider then purchases the requested errand using either business capital provided by the owner. For grocery purchases exceeding three thousand pesos (3000), the rider calculates ten percent (10%) of the fee and adds it to the total cost. For purchases below three thousand pesos, the rider applies a flat fifty-peso (50) commission. When the errand involves multiple stores, the rider adds a thirty-peso (30) fee for each additional stop maxing at three additional stores. These calculations are performed manually by the rider at the point of purchase. After completing the purchase, the rider takes a picture of the            original store receipt using their smartphone camera and sends it to the dispatcher through the messaging application. The rider also sends a photograph of the requested errand to verify that the correct request details were obtained. These images become part of the chat history between the rider and dispatcher.                       With the request complete, the rider transitions to the delivery phase. The rider navigates to the customer’s location using personal knowledge of the area or navigation applications on their phone. The delivery address relies on the landmarks provided in the initial dispatcher message, as many destinations in the service area lack           proper street addressing          . Upon arrival, the rider contacts the customer through a phone call or SMS message to confirm the meet up location. For Cash on Delivery transactions, the rider collects the payment amount which includes the item cost, multiple store fees, and base fee plus (+) additional ten (10) pesos per barangay. The base delivery fee varies depending between the pickup and delivery points, with the dispatcher typically providing an estimate during the initial assignment. The rider records the collected amount either in mind, on paper, or through a personal note on their phone.                       The final operational phase occurs at the end of the business day when the dispatcher and owner conduct settlement procedures. All riders return to their homes and submit their records through the messaging application. The dispatcher collects the original store receipts from each rider and manually cross-references them against the transaction log maintained throughout the day. This transaction log exists as a combination of chat history screenshots, handwritten notes, and in their mind.                        The dispatcher calculates the total revenue by adding all base fees, additional fee for each barangay passed, and multiple store fee collected during the day. From this total, the dispatcher separates the business share from the rider share. The business share includes the commission percentages and surcharges, while the rider share consists of the base fees. This calculation requires the dispatcher to review each transaction individually, verify the receipt images against the recorded amounts,           and fix any errors by directly consulting the riders          . The process typically extends for about three (3+) hours as the team works through the accumulated transactions from the day.                        In the Dispatchers side, it scrolls though an extensive chat history to just to be found. The rider availability is the challenge since the entire operation relies only at home which the coordination is only held in the messenger. Tracking the riders is a challenge, it lacks real-time updates or creating situations where the multiple dispatchers might simultaneously assign tasks to the same rider.                        During the procurement phase, customers occasionally cancel orders after the rider has already purchased the items, resulting in financial loss for the business when the items cannot be returned. During this, a customer also can contact the rider directly without the direct approach to the dispatcher which results also in financial loss because the commission being held to the company is now directly managed by the rider.                       The delivery phase experiences delays when riders misinterpret landmark instructions, requiring phone calls to the dispatcher or customer for clarification.                        The settlement phase presents the most significant challenges, as the unautomated cross-referencing of receipts against transaction records consumes a lot of time for the dispatchers and introduces a calculation error that require next-day corrections as to be performed.                       Riders must perform these calculations while managing the physical errand process, then communicate the totals to customers who may have problem with the charges if they differ from initial estimates. No fixed rate cards or automatic tools, so riders and dispatchers may calculate fees differently. Customer relationship management in the current system relies entirely on the dispatcher's personal knowledge and the chat history archive. Repeat customers must restate their delivery addresses for each new transaction, as the business maintains no centralized database of customer locations or preferences. Verification of customer identity occurs through informal means, with the dispatcher recognizing names or profile pictures from previous interactions. This informal system cannot distinguish between verified regular customers and new accounts that may present payment risks.                        Riders work as independent workers using their own motorcycles and smartphones. Each riders maintain their own record of daily earnings through handwritten tallies or phone            notes, this can cause inconsistencies during payment when their personal records do not match the dispatcher’s transaction log. The rider’s location is the challenge, as the business grows, the rider grows and the dispatcher cannot simply monitor these. Lack of real-time visibility of the rider’s location is the concern.                       The dispatcher assigns tasks based on estimated distance instead of exact location data, so a rider who is actually closer to the pickup point may be skipped in favor of another rider who recently said they are available. This method depends on the dispatcher’s memory of where riders are instead of real-time location tracking.                       The records for each transaction are spread out in different messages and screenshots across several chats. Customer request is in the main Facebook page inbox, while rider assignments are in private messages, and receipts are in each rider’s chat history. Since no single place has the complete details, the dispatcher has to check multiple chats to verify everything during payment settlement.                       Picture proof of delivery occurs inconsistently, with some riders sending confirmation images while others simply report completion through text messages. This documentation gap creates difficulties when customers have conflict with delivery claims or report missing items, as the business cannot always provide conclusive evidence of successful completion.                       The transition from messenger-based operation to a structured digital platform represents the current strategic focus of the business. The existing workflow has supported nearly six years of operation but now constrains growth as transaction volumes increase and customer expectations for service speed rise. To address these limitations, Sugo On-the-Go System, a mobile-based errand service management system, is being developed to simplify and digitize the core operational processes of Sugo Express On the Go Logistics Services.                       The manual process that worked for about Fifty (50) transactions a day is now too heavy to handle one hundred fifty (150) transactions, causing the dispatcher and rider to work overtime, especially during settlement. The Sugo On-the-Go System solves this by automatically recording transactions, managing dispatch digitally, and generating settlement summaries, removing the need for manual counting and reducing extra            operating costs.                                    As the number of customers grows, the informal way of identifying customers is no longer reliable, and there are also no real-time tracking of riders and their tasks, making it hard to manage delays. The system fixes this by adding customer registration and verification, so each customer has a recognized profile, and a real-time tracking feature so dispatchers and the owner can monitor rider location and order status anytime for better and faster decision-making.                       These targeted system capabilities establish Sugo On-the-Go System as the direct operational response to the baseline constraints from which all system improvements will be measured. Review of Related Literature                       Errand Service Management                         Digital platforms currently form the cornerstone of what is referred to as 'platform economics,' progressively assuming a central role in the city's economy, experiential landscape, and governance. On-demand platforms play a pivotal role in elevating the level of digital sophistication among citizens (Popescu, A. 2024).                        The evolution of on-demand errands began with the concept of "neighbors helping neighbors." The industry’s modern iteration was sparked in 2008 with the founding of TaskRabbit, which utilized a bidding system for local chores. Early literature emphasizes that these platforms were initially seen as a way to monetize "spare time" rather than a primary source of income (Busque 2022).                        The landscape of on-demand errand systems in the Philippines has transitioned from simple delivery services to "Agentic Mobility" ecosystems. As of 2026, these systems are a cornerstone of the Philippine digital economy, driven by hyper-localization and the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) to navigate the country’s unique logistical challenges.                        On-demand errand systems in the Philippines are growing rapidly, driven by the gig economy, smartphone adoption, and a demand for convenience in urban areas, particularly Metro Manila. These platforms, often operating as mobile-based apps, provide services such as courier, personal shopping, and cleaning, significantly impacting urban mobility and livelihood by offering flexible work options.                             History of Errand Service Management                         Historically, errand services were defined by the role of the "messenger" or "errand boy." Research indicates that demand for these in-house roles peaked around 1945 before declining in the 1960s due to changing organizational structures and labor supply (Håkansson et al., 2022). Initially, large organizations employed youth (typically aged 15–17) to handle deliveries and office tasks, but as the labor market restructured, these roles            shifted from formal employment to more varied service occupations (Håkansson et al., 2022).                        The evolution of Errand Service Management, specifically within the realms of food delivery and "pabili" (purchasing services), represents a transition from informal community favors to a sophisticated, multi-billion-dollar technological industry. In the Philippine context, these services are deeply rooted in a culture of communal assistance and mutual aid, which has historically facilitated the exchange of goods and services (Pelegrina et al., 2024). This historical trajectory began in ancient times with localized food systems, such as the pre-colonial Barangay system, where Datus managed the distribution of resources, as well as global precedents like Italy’s first recorded pizza delivery in 1889 and India’s highly organized Dabbawala network established in the 1890s (KitchenHub, 2023; Scribd, 2023).                                     Between the 1950s and the 1990s, the sector entered the era of traditional management, characterized by the rise of infrastructure and the integration of telephone-based ordering systems. During this period, major food chains like Jollibee and McDonald's established in-house delivery fleets, while logistics pioneers like LBC provided the foundational framework for modern last-mile delivery services in the Philippines (KitchenHub, 2023; Softlink Global, 2024). By the 2010s, the industry underwent a digital revolution with the emergence of "Super Apps." Platforms such as Foodpanda and Grab transformed errand management into an algorithm-driven economy, normalizing third-party logistics and popularizing digitized "pabili" services that utilize motorcycle fleets for diverse tasks (Grab, 2024; KitchenHub, 2023).                                     The COVID-19 pandemic acted as a "great accelerator" for the errand economy, driving a shift toward hyper-local management and contactless innovation. As mobility was restricted, community-based "pasabuy" groups flourished on social media to address essential needs (Pelegrina et al., 2024). This period solidified the necessity of management            innovation, with providers integrating real-time tracking and cashless payments to enhance safety and efficiency (Softlink Global, 2024). By 2024, the industry will have matured into a sophisticated ecosystem utilizing artificial intelligence for demand forecasting and route optimization. Ultimately, this progression marks a transition from labor-intensive, hierarchical models to scalable, technology-driven platforms (World Bank, 2021).                        The emergence of the gig economy in the early 21st century significantly transformed errand services from exclusive offerings into accessible, on-demand solutions for the general public. A notable example is TaskRabbit, founded in 2008 (originally as RunMyErrand) by Leah Busque during the Great Recession. This platform pioneered a digital marketplace model that connects freelance workers with local demand. Initially focusing on simple tasks such as grocery shopping, furniture assembly, and delivery services, the platform allowed users to outsource everyday chores conveniently. As it expanded, TaskRabbit introduced mobile applications that enabled real-time task posting and acceptance, demonstrating how technology could efficiently match supply and demand in localized service markets (Sundararajan, 2016).                       The rapid expansion of the gig economy led to the diversification of errand services into specialized categories such as grocery delivery, laundry services, document courier services, and home cleaning. The widespread adoption of smartphones enabled key technological advancements, including real-time GPS tracking, instant communication between service providers and clients, and seamless digital payment integration. These developments eliminated many inefficiencies associated with traditional service models, particularly those related to coordination delays and cash handling (Kenney &amp; Zysman, 2016).                             Comparison of Mobile-Based and Web-Based Errand Management Systems                       The technological landscape of service logistics is increasingly defined by the choice between mobile-based and web-based architectures, each offering distinct advantages for errand management. According to research by Davila et al. (2003), web-based systems provide a robust framework for administrative oversight, offering a centralized "thin-client" interface that is accessible from any standard browser. These platforms are            particularly effective for complex scheduling, fleet management, and deep data analytics, as they allow dispatchers to view large-scale operational dashboards that are often difficult to navigate on smaller screens. Because web systems are hosted on central servers, they ensure that all stakeholders—from office managers to suppliers—are working with real-time data synchronization, reducing the risk of administrative errors in high-volume transaction environments (Pani &amp; Agrahari, 2007).                       However, the specific nature of errand services, which requires high mobility and "on-the-ground" responsiveness, often favors mobile-based systems for field operations. Mobile applications leverage device-specific features such as Global Positioning System (GPS) tracking, camera integration for proof of delivery, and push notifications for instant task alerts. As noted by Brandon-Jones and Carey (2011), the primary benefit of a mobile-first approach is the reduction of latency in communication. Theory of Relational Database Management System                        The Theory of Databases has a broad range of topics related to the study and research of the theoretical realm of databases and database management systems.                        A database is a repository of data, stored as a table made up of masses of data that have some connection to each other. The database is organized in that it uses records and fields so that the data is easier to use, whether it’s to analyze, add, delete, or otherwise manipulate it. Additionally, a database is structured so that it is clear how the data points within it relate to one another. The structure of the data allows it to be accessible as needed by users and computer programs (Goyal, 2023).                        Modern database theory has transitioned from the strict reliability of relational models toward more elastic, distributed frameworks to handle the complexities of modern data. The foundation of the field lies in E.F. Codd’s relational model, which relies on ACID properties to guarantee absolute data integrity and consistency (IBM, 2024; Wikipedia, 2026). As data volume increased, the NoSQL movement introduced the BASE paradigm, trading immediate consistency for the horizontal scalability and high availability required by large-scale systems (Kurve, 2026; Mester, 2025). This architectural evolution is largely shaped by the CAP Theorem, which forces a choice between consistency and availability in the face of network partitions (Kurve, 2026). Consequently, the current landscape increasingly favors NewSQL and polyglot persistence strategies, which integrate traditional relational rigor with the flexibility of modern distributed systems            (ResearchGate, 2024). Theory of Non-relational Database Management System                       The theoretical framework of Non-relational Database Management Systems (NoSQL) is primarily rooted in the CAP Theorem and a shift toward flexible data modeling to address the limitations of traditional relational systems in handling Big Data.                       NoSQL database theory is centered on the CAP Theorem, which states that distributed systems must balance three competing needs: consistency, availability, and partition tolerance (Oprea et al., 2023). To achieve high availability and the ability to "scale out" across multiple servers, NoSQL systems often swap strict, real-time consistency for eventual consistency (Oprea et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2017). Unlike traditional databases with rigid structures, NoSQL uses a schema-on-read approach, allowing data to be stored in flexible formats like Key-Value pairs, documents, columns, or graphs (Klettke et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2017). This flexibility enables lazy schema evolution, where data is updated only when needed, making it ideal for handling massive amounts of unstructured "Big Data" (Klettke et al., 2016). While this provides great speed and agility, it can make complex queries difficult, leading many modern researchers to use hybrid models that combine NoSQL’s scalability with the reliability of traditional SQL systems (Sen &amp; Mukherjee, 2023; Wu et al., 2017). This functional foundation enables JavaScript’s signature event-driven architecture, where the execution of code is triggered by user interactions or system events rather than a simple top-down sequence. Unlike many traditional languages that use class-based inheritance, JavaScript’s prototype-based theory allows objects to inherit properties directly from other objects, offering a highly flexible and dynamic approach to data modeling (Flanagan, 2020A significant point of discussion in the literature centers on JavaScript’s execution model and its "just-in-time" (JIT) compilation theory. As a dynamically typed language, JavaScript performs type checking during runtime, which Richards et al. Peopleware: The Users of the System                                                  Table 6 describes the human roles within the system, emphasizing how each contributes to the system’s effectiveness and efficiency. The Owner oversees the overall operations, monitors daily errand activities, evaluates rider performance, and approves financial reports including Sugo and Rider shares and guiding strategic decisions for business growth. The Dispatcher handles incoming errand requests, assigns tasks to available riders, verifies customer information and landmarks, monitors riders’ real-time status, and ensures smooth communication between riders and customers to guarantee timely deliveries. Delivery Staff (Riders) accept errands through the mobile app, follow digital waybills, purchase requested items, deliver orders to customers, upload proof of purchase, and update errand statuses in real time to maintain transparency and accountability. The Developer designs and implements the system, ensures proper integration and functionality, and maintains the platform after deployment to enhance operational efficiency and user experience. Together, these roles form a coordinated team that supports smooth errand execution, accurate data management, and reliable communication within the system. Network Design                                     Figure 2 shows the network design of the proposed Mobile-based Errand Service Management System for Sugo Express On the Go Logistics Services. The diagram illustrates how the different users of the system are connected through router and mobile data to enable communication, access to the internet service provider inside and outside, data processing internally, real-time data processing externally, and accessing data externally within the network coverage or outside. The owner, dispatcher, and rider use their respective devices, while the dispatcher uses a computer/laptop to access and accepts customer request in the system. Additionally, the dispatcher PC devices for productivity and accessibility. All of these inside the diagram are linked through router’s office. Through the use of router, the devices were able to communicate with each other and access the internet for web-based operations. Moreover, the other diagram shows the customer showing that the system can interact to the customers that provide service to them. This setup supports the idea of well-structured mobile/web application which, according to Liu et al (2019). The utilization of mobile devices in all the users and administrative staff supports the finding of Karim et al. (2006) high readiness and positive perception toward the implementation of mobile-based information services.                       The internet will connect to the system to the Google Firebase Services as Database and a Virtual Server for MySQL database, which is responsible for storing, managing, retrieving data such as transaction purchase, providing real-time data accessible, billing information, customer data, and other system records. As stated by Alzahrani &amp; Samma (2019), comparing these two database systems. It describes a hybrid or comparative architecture where the system leverages both Firebase (for real-time access) and MySQL (for relational data management). This network design allows authorized users and customer users to access the system based on their role and responsibilities and use, ensuring a more organized, efficient, and a source of truth of information.                       The Google Firebase Services serves as the alternative database for fast storing, managing, and retrieving small information into the system. This database is responsible for retrieving small information that the system currently must optimize the performance and to handle the bottleneck of the main MySQL server. This database will retrieve information and            delivery them in real-time into the system. Scrum Methodology                                                  The development process will follow an incremental strategy, delivering functional system components in successive phases to ensure steady progress while other elements are being finalized. Rather than aiming for a single final release, researchers will iteratively build and enhance specific modules. This approach prioritizes core functionality, allowing for early evaluation and using feedback to guide subsequent improvements. By adopting this modular progression, the project maintains high flexibility and fosters continuous refinement throughout the lifecycle. The design will ensure data integrity, security, and scalability to handle approximately one hundred fifty daily transactions and thirty-eight active riders. Current errand process                                     Figure 4. This illustrates the Sugo: Services On the Go, The workflow centers on a three-way coordination between the Customer, a Dispatcher, and the Delivery Rider. The dispatcher acts as the central hub, managing requests via computer system to bridge the gap between the client's needs and available personnel. The process relies heavily on mobile interface interactions. Key data points such as order lists, location markers, and messaging are exchanged. This is represented by various icons indicating status updates: Task completion or verification, Notification of delays or issues, and Real-time chat between the runner and the customer for specific instructions. The final stage shows the synchronization between the user (requesting the service) and the worker (executing the task). Their interaction is mediated by the app’s interface, ensuring transparency and accountability throughout the duration of the errand.                                                                                                          Figure                       SEQ Figure \* ARABIC                       5                      . Delivery and pick-up for errand task (Pabili)                                     Figure 5. This illustrates the Sugo: Services On the Go, The process begins at a commercial location (such as a restaurant or store). The errand runner arrives at the source to pick up the requested items. This stage involves a transaction where the runner pays for the goods on behalf of the client, ensuring the order is correct before departing. The runner, depicted on a scooter, initiates the delivery phase. This represents the real-time movement of the goods through the local infrastructure. During this stage, the runner is responsible for the safety and timely transport of the items. Upon arriving at the customer's residence, the runner initiates the final hand-off. The customer receives the package and performs a digital transaction. Using a mobile payment device or payment, the customer settles the service fee and reimbursement, marking the errand as successfully completed. The three architectures include the following:                           Presentation Tier                           Application Tier Database Tier                           Database Tier                       Data is saved in the database by the system’s users using the three-tier architecture. Users are responsible for handling both interfaces and application layers while the system oversees handling the database layer.
